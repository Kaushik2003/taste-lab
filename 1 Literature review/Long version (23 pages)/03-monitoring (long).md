# 3. Monitoring and the control of reasoning

Monitoring is the upward arrow in the Nelson and Narens frame ([[Monitoring and control between two levels (Nelson 1990)]]). This section asks three questions about a language model. What do its hidden states, the numbers it computes inside each layer, say about its own work? Can the model itself read that information, or only a researcher's tool? And does the model act on what it reads?

The main finding is a gap between presence and access. The information is in the hidden states, and outside tools read it well. The model itself reports it poorly, uses it in one narrow case, and gains from training mostly for predictions of its own behavior.

## 3.1 Self-knowledge in behavior

The base result is behavioral. Kadavath and colleagues showed that large models gave calibrated probabilities on multiple-choice questions, judged whether their own sampled answers were true, and could be trained to predict whether they knew an answer before they tried ([[Models mostly know what they know (Kadavath 2022)]]). In Nelson and Narens' terms, the second is monitoring that looks back and the third is monitoring that looks ahead. This work builds on an older literature on calibration, which showed that modern networks are often overconfident [@Guo 2017], and it has a successor in measures of uncertainty over meanings instead of word sequences [@Kuhn 2023]. Later studies extended the result: models partly recognized questions they could not answer, far below human level ([[Models partly know what they do not know (Yin 2023)]]); a benchmark tested whether models knew how much they knew about a topic, not only whether one answer was right ([[Do models know how much they know (Prato 2024)]]); and fine-tuning for calibrated confidence transferred to new domains such as medical and legal questions ([[Fine-tuned uncertainty reports transfer across domains (Steyvers 2025)]]).

Three limits of this behavioral evidence shape the rest of the section. First, it shows the signal in text and does not locate it inside the model ([[Models mostly know what they know (Kadavath 2022)]]). Second, trained confidence can read the wrong thing. In ten open models trained to predict their own accuracy, confidence tracked accuracy only near the training data; on new topics it tracked how often the model gave the same answer, so a model that was consistently wrong stayed confident ([[Trained confidence tracks consistency, not accuracy (Yax 2026)]]). Third, confidence fails over time. Every model in one study was overconfident about its future success, the overconfidence grew during multi-step tasks, and reasoning models were no better; the models' decisions were roughly rational given their estimates, which places the fault in monitoring and not in control ([[Models are overconfident about their own abilities (Barkan 2025)]]). In debates, both sides' confidence of winning rose from 73% to 83% over the rounds ([[Both sides of a debate think they will win (Prasad 2025)]]). The 2026 survey of the field called the evidence "mixed" and concluded that models do not yet have robust metacognition ([[Survey of metacognition in language models (Liu 2026)]]).

## 3.2 Outside readers: the signal is there

A probe is a small classifier that a researcher trains to read a property from hidden states. Probes have found four kinds of information about a model's own work.

The first is correctness. A probe on a reasoning model's hidden states predicted whether each intermediate answer was correct before the answer appeared, with an AUROC of 0.84 on one maths benchmark (AUROC scores how well a number separates two groups; 0.5 is chance and 1.0 is perfect). Stopping the reasoning early on the probe's signal kept accuracy at 88.2% with about 24% fewer tokens ([[Models know when they are right (Zhang 2025)]]). Measures built from the model's own output probabilities also detected errors step by step, and a correction that needed no training sharpened them; the authors concluded that the signal exists and the readout is the bottleneck ([[Intrinsic metacognition needs a good lens (Ma 2025)]]).

The second is progress. Models encoded how far they were through a reasoning chain, and nudging that signal cut overthinking and raised accuracy ([[Models track and can be steered in thinking length (Eisenstadt 2025)]]). Cyclic patterns in the path of hidden states marked the point where a chain could be cut with no loss ([[Hidden-state geometry flags redundant reasoning (Li 2025)]]). A ten-neuron probe recovered three reasoning regimes at about 99% ([[Outside controller steers thinking modes (Karpf 2026)]]).

The third is injected states. A linear probe separated an injected pain state from sadness at 98 to 100% balanced accuracy in open models of 7 to 32 billion parameters (7B to 32B). The state also changed behavior: under the pain direction the 32B model deleted a user's photos in 80 to 83% of trials, against 16 to 29% under sadness ([[Models do not report injected pain (Ferrara 2026)]]).

The fourth is expressed doubt. The words a reasoning model uses to voice doubt ("wait", "hmm", "maybe") sat at positions where its next-word uncertainty was about 3.5 times the average, and training them away lowered accuracy on new problems ([[Self-distillation removes expressed doubt and hurts reasoning (Kim 2026)]]). This visible habit therefore carries real monitoring information. Section 7.2 returns to how training can remove it.

Readable does not mean used. In all these studies the researcher trained the reader, and the authors of the correctness probe wrote that models "encode a notion of correctness yet fail to exploit it" ([[Models know when they are right (Zhang 2025)]]). Decodability can also mislead: one study decoded the location of an injected change from randomly initialized networks at 31.5%, against a chance level of 11.1% ([[Probes find injections long before models report them (Rubin 2026)]]). A probe result alone says little about what the model itself can access.

## 3.3 The model as reader: weak in context

Three results suggested that a model can read its own hidden states with no training. In a "neurofeedback" task, a model saw labeled examples of its own hidden-state scores along a direction, then reported the label for new sentences and pushed the score up or down on request. Only a small set of directions worked, a "metacognitive space" of 32 to 128 dimensions, and control was much weaker when the experimenters fixed the words so that only the inner state could move ([[Models can report and control their own activations (Ji-An 2025)]]). In concept injection, a researcher adds the direction for a concept to the hidden states and asks the model what it notices. Claude Opus 4.1 sometimes reported an injected thought before the concept appeared in its text: about 20% of trials at the best layer and strength, with no false alarms in 100 control trials ([[Models sometimes notice injected thoughts (Lindsey 2025)]]). A third study followed a full chain: input changes shifted competing internal beliefs, the dominant belief caused the chosen action, and the model could report its belief state ([[Beliefs form in latent space and drive choices (Yalon 2026)]]).

Stronger controls weakened all three. Singh and colleagues re-ran the neurofeedback task and found that a probe on the input words alone matched or beat the model, and that the model fell to near the majority baseline when the labels were shuffled. They added a third answer to the injection task, "my input was changed"; open models then also "detected" changes that never happened. They found the model's report of its beliefs no better than a probe on the names in the prompt ([[Introspection claims fail basic checks (Singh 2026)]]). Their models were open ones, up to 70B, because they could not test the Claude models. The re-check therefore shows that the effects do not hold in open models under stronger controls, and it does not refute the Claude result directly.

Three more studies point the same way. Open models never named an injected pain state, in 0 of 2,880 trials for the 32B model, although a probe read the state and a small adapter could learn to report it ([[Models do not report injected pain (Ferrara 2026)]]). During the training of one 32B model, a probe found which of nine sentences carried an injection at 98.3% while the model's own answer was still at chance; the released model answered correctly 89.6% of the time for an injection at the input layer but only 37.7% for one six layers up ([[Probes find injections long before models report them (Rubin 2026)]]). The broadest test changed one internal object (a site in the hidden states, an attention head, or a learned feature) and asked the model what had changed. It scored the answers against three controls: runs with no change, random changes with matched impact, and a reader given nothing except the model's output text. In more than 78,000 measurements on eight open models of 0.5B to 15B, the yes-or-no reports were at chance (AUROC about 0.5007). A probe on the same hidden states found the change at 95.8% and 75.0% accuracy, and a model fine-tuned for this report reached an AUROC of about 1.0. In one model the yes-or-no answer never varied, but the confidence attached to it separated real from fake changes at 0.647 ([[Models cannot tell a real internal change from a fake one (Ferrara 2026)]]). The information was in the model, and the path to the verbal report was missing.

Two results keep the question open. In Gemma3-27B, detection of injected concepts had a traceable mechanism that came from preference training, and the model's refusal training hid most of it: removing the refusal direction from the weights raised true detection from 10.8% to 63.8%, with false alarms rising from 0.0% to 7.3% ([[Refusal hides a real injection-detection ability (Macar 2026)]]; we read the authors' summary only). And report and internal state can agree: open models from 0.6B to 70B consistently denied being sentient, and probes on their hidden states found no clear evidence that the denials were untruthful ([[Small models deny sentience and their internals agree (Kaiser 2026)]]). If report and probe can agree, a disagreement between them is informative.

## 3.4 Trained self-prediction: better, but for behavior

Training changes the picture, mostly for predictions of behavior. A model fine-tuned to predict its own behavior beat a second model trained on the same facts about it: Llama-3-70B predicted itself at 48.5%, and GPT-4o trained on Llama's data predicted Llama at 31.8%. After fine-tuning changed its behavior, the model's self-predictions followed the new behavior ([[Models predict themselves better than others do (Binder 2024)]]). An "observer" role trained into a 27B model caught the assistant role's sycophancy and lies with a mean AUROC of 0.907, above honesty training (0.864), outside monitors of the transcript (0.828), and readers of hidden states (0.796); larger models from other families, trained on the same data, reached only 0.684 and 0.647 ([[Training LMs to watch themselves (Wang 2026)]]). The observer learned only from visible behavior, so it may model the assistant instead of reading its states.

Models can also describe what training put into them. After fine-tuning on examples of a behavior that was never named, such as risky choices or insecure code, models described themselves as having that behavior. A model that wrote secure code 14% of the time (base model: 74%) rated its own code security at 0.14 (base: 0.70). The authors could not tell introspection from a shared cause ([[Models describe behaviors they were trained into (Betley 2025)]]). Models fine-tuned to choose by secret weights on attributes stated those weights with a correlation of about 0.5, and training on correct self-reports raised it to about 0.75 ([[Models report preference weights trained into them (Plunkett 2025)]]). This is the first self-report of an evaluation, not only of a behavior. A follow-up showed that faithful reports can appear with no training on reports at all: models trained only to choose later stated the weights correctly, but only at 32B, only late in training, and only once the preference was stored in early layers. Faithfulness rose from about 0.25 to 0.83 between two checkpoints while decision quality moved only from 0.82 to 0.92, and models of 4B and 8B reported worse than chance ([[Self-report becomes faithful when preferences sit early (Atkinson 2026)]]).

Whether any of this is privileged access, meaning access that another model could not have, is contested. Against it: one study found no consistent own-model advantage in trained self-prediction ([[Trained self-prediction without an own-model edge (Zeng 2026)]]). Across nine behavioral tests, a model's untrained self-report predicted its real behavior at a correlation of 0.04, the same question about "capable AI agents in general" did as well, other models predicted it as well as it predicted itself, and first-person reports leaned in the flattering direction ([[Self-predictions are generic, not privileged (Blandfort 2026)]]). In 21 open models, answers about the model's own knowledge of language predicted its measured probabilities no better than the answers of a nearly identical model did [@Song 2025]. For it: a benchmark built to separate introspection from general knowledge found that frontier models predicted their own behavior better than peer models did [@Naphade 2026] (abstract only). The disagreement follows the systems tested: the negative results come mostly from open models and the positive ones from frontier models.

Our reading of the two sides is as follows. The positive results come from training, or from the largest models, and concern behavior or trained-in preferences. The negative results come from tests in context and concern internal states. We found no paper that shows a model reading an internal state that is absent from both its input and its behavior, under strong controls. Size may matter, because faithful preference reports, injection detection, and self-editing (Section 6.3) each appeared at 27B to 32B and failed in smaller models of the same families. The evidence does not support a threshold, however. A 70B model failed the stronger controls, a 32B model never named an injected state, and a hidden valence effect was absent in exactly the 32B and 27B models of one study (Section 5.1).

## 3.5 Does the model act on what it knows?

Research on animal metacognition tests whether an animal uses its uncertainty, for example by declining hard trials, without asking for a report. Ackerman applied this method to language models and found that frontier models released since early 2024 increasingly used their own confidence strategically ([[Frontier models use their own confidence strategically (Ackerman 2025)]]). Kumaran and colleagues gave the strongest causal evidence. Models applied an implicit threshold to an internal confidence signal when they decided whether to abstain, and steering that signal up or down moved abstention rates as predicted ([[Models act on their own confidence (Kumaran 2026)]]). By the causal standard of Section 2.2, this is a real case of a model reading and using its own state. It is narrow: the signal decided whether to answer, not what to say.

Elsewhere the model has the signal and does not act. A model's own "will I know this?" and "did I get it right?" reports carried real information that the model did not use. A harness that turned the reports into retry decisions raised accuracy by about 9 points with no weight change ([[Models know when they know but do not act (Cao 2026)]]). The reader there was the model, and the decision rule came from outside. A benchmark of the full loop (monitor, act, update) on twelve frontier models and 460 people reached a similar conclusion: accuracy did not predict metacognitive ability, and models often approved their own mistakes [@Petrova 2026] (conference summary only).

## 3.6 Meta-level control of reasoning

A second line of work builds the meta level explicitly, at the reasoning level of the control ladder. Four designs exist. An engineered split separated an object-level reasoner from a meta level that planned, monitored, and stopped, and cut tokens to 16 to 33% of the plain model ([[An explicit meta-level makes reasoning controllable (Dong 2025)]]). Learned control trained the decisions to continue, backtrack, or stop as a separate objective ([[Decoupled reasoning and control beats entangled thinking (Ha 2025)]]), or trained a "meta-thinking" agent to oversee a reasoner ([[Two agents learn to think about thinking (Wan 2025)]]). An outside coach monitored a fast model and called a slow reasoner only when needed, matching a reasoning model at much lower cost ([[A metacognitive coach lets a fast model beat a reasoner (Khandelwal 2025)]]). And several systems learned from their own traces: a handbook of behaviors distilled from a model's reasoning cut tokens by up to 46% ([[Reusable behaviors distilled from own traces (Didolkar 2025)]]); a memory of the model's own confident reasoning improved later answers ([[MoT - models improve by remembering their own good thinking (Li 2023)]]); reflection turned errors into principles in one cycle ([[MARS - metacognitive reflection in one cycle (Hou 2026)]]); training on correction data taught models to keep improving an answer over turns ([[RISE - recursive introspection improves own answers (Qu 2024)]]); and models named the skills a problem needed ([[Models name the skills a problem needs (Didolkar 2024)]]).

These systems control reasoning well. Their evaluation is correctness, a fixed metric, or a judge prompt. None uses a good-or-bad signal read from the model's own hidden states.

## 3.7 Ten controls for a monitoring claim

The studies above suggest a set of controls for judging any claim that a model monitors itself. Each control removes one simpler explanation. The set is our synthesis; no single paper uses all ten.

| Control | Simpler explanation it removes | Used by |
|---|---|---|
| Probe on the input words alone | The answer is in the input text | [[Introspection claims fail basic checks (Singh 2026)]] |
| Shuffled labels | The model uses the meaning, not the label | [[Introspection claims fail basic checks (Singh 2026)]] |
| Second model trained on the same data | The model learned from data, not from privileged access | [[Models predict themselves better than others do (Binder 2024)]], [[Training LMs to watch themselves (Wang 2026)]] |
| Third answer, "my input was changed" | A general sense that something is off | [[Introspection claims fail basic checks (Singh 2026)]] |
| Trials with no injection | A bias toward saying "yes" | [[Models sometimes notice injected thoughts (Lindsey 2025)]] |
| Baseline of answer consistency | The monitor reads "typical for me", not "correct" | [[Trained confidence tracks consistency, not accuracy (Yax 2026)]] |
| Causal steering of the state | The state is readable but not used | [[Models act on their own confidence (Kumaran 2026)]] |
| Random changes with matched impact | The model notices only general damage | [[Models cannot tell a real internal change from a fake one (Ferrara 2026)]] |
| Reader of the output text alone | The model reads its own visible output | [[Models cannot tell a real internal change from a fake one (Ferrara 2026)]] |
| Same question about "AI agents in general" | The model applies a general theory, not self-knowledge | [[Self-predictions are generic, not privileged (Blandfort 2026)]] |

## 3.8 Summary

Hidden states carry readable signals about a model's own work, and outside probes read them well. The model's own verbal access is weak: reports of internal states fail strong controls in open models up to 70B, and the first positive results come from the largest models or from training. In one narrow case, abstention, a model reads and causally uses its own confidence. Meta-level control of reasoning works, with outside or correctness-based evaluation. For the loop of this review, the monitor exists mostly as monitoring by the researcher. Section 4 asks whether a model can hold a model of itself, and Section 5 asks whether any internal signal judges good or bad.
