# 1. Introduction

Reasoning models now do something that looks, from the outside, like self-knowledge. They hesitate, backtrack, say "wait", and verify their own steps. This review asks a sharper question underneath that behavior: can a reasoning model **monitor** its own internal states, **evaluate** them with likes and dislikes of its own - a taste - and use that evaluation to **control** its thinking and, eventually, its own weights? And if it can, is that taste **self-originated**: does it drift away from the human preferences it was trained on because the model keeps selecting for it?

The question is no longer fringe. A 2025 review treats AI awareness - metacognition, self-awareness, social awareness - as a measurable functional capacity rather than philosophy ([[AI awareness as a measurable capacity (Li 2025)]]), and a Trends in Cognitive Sciences manifesto with Bengio, Mitchell, Rahwan, Scholkopf, Chater and Gerstenberg among its authors argues that AI has become smart without becoming wise, and that the missing ingredient is exactly metacognition: intellectual humility, perspective-taking, context adaptability ([[Wise machines need metacognition not just smarts (Johnson 2025)]]). The question matters for two more reasons. Scientifically, metacognition is one of the oldest frameworks in psychology ([[Monitoring and control between two levels (Nelson 1990)]]), and LLMs are the first artificial systems where its pieces can be measured directly, because the internal states are readable. Practically, a model that can tell good from bad in its own work, without an external judge, could regulate its reasoning, choose what to learn, and improve on qualities nobody wrote a reward function for. Human oversight also has limits: users systematically over-trust what models know ([[People over-trust what models know (Steyvers 2025)]]), merely receiving model advice inflates confidence in both the model and oneself ([[AI advice inflates confidence in the AI and in oneself (Colombatto 2025)]]), and the user's own self-assessment degrades with AI use ([[AI use flattens Dunning-Kruger and breaks self-assessment (Fernandes 2025)]]). These findings motivate stronger model-side monitoring; they do not show that human oversight can be replaced.

The organizing structure of this review is a loop: **monitor -> evaluate -> control -> persist**, with an **anchor** against collapse. Monitoring asks what a model's hidden states say about its own work. Evaluation asks which internal signals could serve as the good/bad judgment. Control asks how that judgment changes behavior, from choosing among outputs to steering activations. Persist asks how a preference gets written into weights. The anchor asks what keeps a loop that runs on its own signal from sharpening into collapse.

A taste counts as self-originated only if it passes four tests ([[Four tests for own taste]]): it is **divergent** from human and training preferences, **stable** across paraphrase and context, **grounded** in a probed internal state rather than surface self-report, and **self-reinforcing** under self-training without collapse. Each test alone has a cheap explanation; together they are the bar.

Throughout, the testbed is **code readability among programs matched on observed test outcomes**. Passing the same test suite constrains functional behavior but does not establish complete correctness equivalence or remove performance and robustness differences. The gating question in [[Q1 Does the model sense code quality]] is whether an internal valence readout tracks blind human readability judgments beyond length, perplexity and comment density.

**Bounded contribution.** We propose to test whether a causally validated activation-level evaluation readout can guide output selection and subsequent self-training for readability among programs matched on observed test outcomes, with held-out audits of quality and drift. This is a proposed experimental contribution, not a claim that quality evaluation or self-rewarding is absent from the literature: [[Self-rewarding language models judge and train themselves (Yuan 2024)]] already uses a model's own verbal quality judgments for training, and [[Human preference is weakly readable from hidden states (Kirin 2026)]] reads human preference from activations. Section 8 separates those precedents from the proposed combination.

The review proceeds along the loop. Section 2 gives the foundations. Sections 3 to 7 cover monitoring, evaluation signals, persistent control, the limits of self-referential training, and the measurement of taste. Section 8 assembles the pieces, states where the field stops, presents the strongest evidence against the thesis, and draws the architecture-level implication: the components for an agent that develops its own metacognition - persistent self-state ([[A persistent latent stream gives metacognitive behavior (Aviss 2025)]]), self-modeling in a world model ([[MUSE - a world model that predicts its own failure (Valiente 2024)]]), learned self-modification ([[Nested Learning - a model that learns its own update rule (Behrouz 2025)]]) - exist as separate papers, and their combination, gated by an internal evaluation signal, is the longer-term agenda this review names G5, not a demonstrated contribution of the proposed readability experiment. Section 9 concludes with what can defensibly be claimed and the experiments that follow.
